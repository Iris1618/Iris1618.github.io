<div class="nibs-page nibs-content"><p class=meta>Rui Lin Lab · NIBS, Beijing | Summer Research Intern | June–August 2026</p><p>Two summer projects combining microglia transplantation, tissue analysis, and in vivo imaging.</p><section class=project><div class=num>PROJECT 01</div><h2>Establish whole-brain microglial gene editing</h2><p class=approach><em>Primary Microglia Transplantation (pMGT) into Adult Mice</em></p><p>This project explored primary microglia transplantation into adult mice as an approach toward whole-brain microglial gene editing. The workflow combined primary cell isolation and culture, AAV-mediated editing, and transplantation, followed by tissue staining and flow cytometry to evaluate engraftment.</p><h3>Workflow</h3><figure class=workflow><img src=/images/nibs/workflow-transplant.png alt='Workflow from the internship PPT: microglia culture, gene editing, and transplantation'></figure><h3>Results · Flow Cytometry and Tissue Imaging</h3><p>BLZ, a CSF1R inhibitor, was used to reduce resident microglia and create space for transplanted cells. The dosing comparison examined how different treatment conditions affected engraftment readouts; the sections below show the 160 mg/kg BLZ condition. This was an exploratory comparison rather than an established optimal dose.</p><figure><img src=/images/nibs/flow-results.png alt='Flow cytometry plots with sample file metadata removed'><figcaption>Day 9 flow cytometry · Left to right: 160 mg/kg, 200 mg/kg, and food-based treatment. GFP-positive events within the displayed gates were approximately 2.89%, 9.55%, and 0.92%, respectively.</figcaption></figure><figure class=tissue><div class=tissue-label><strong>BLZ · 160 mg/kg · Day 9</strong></div><div class=tissue-channels><figure><div class=channel>GFP · Green</div><img src=/images/nibs/sagittal-160-gfp.png alt='GFP fluorescence in a sagittal section, BLZ 160 mg/kg'></figure><figure><div class=channel>IBA1 · Red</div><img src=/images/nibs/sagittal-160-iba1.png alt='IBA1 fluorescence in a sagittal section, BLZ 160 mg/kg'></figure><figure><div class=channel>Merge</div><img src=/images/nibs/sagittal-160-merge.png alt='Merged GFP and IBA1 fluorescence, BLZ 160 mg/kg'></figure></div><figcaption>Representative sagittal sections used to assess transplanted cells alongside flow cytometry.</figcaption></figure><div class=contribution><strong>My contributions</strong><ul><li>Performed primary microglia isolation and culture and AAV-mediated gene editing.</li><li>Carried out oral gavage and stereotaxic injections as part of the transplantation experiments.</li><li>Conducted mouse perfusion, brain sectioning, immunofluorescence staining, and flow cytometry to evaluate microglial engraftment.</li></ul><strong>What I learned</strong><p>I gained experience across the workflow from cell preparation to tissue-level evaluation. Comparing tissue imaging with flow cytometry helped me understand how sampling and low target-event counts can affect interpretation, and why complementary readouts matter.</p></div></section><section class=project><div class=num>PROJECT 02</div><h2>Investigate CD39 regulation of injury-induced ATP signaling</h2><p class=approach><em>CD39 overexpression (CD39-OE) with two-photon imaging</em></p><p>This project investigated the role of CD39 in injury-induced ATP signaling using CD39 overexpression and in vivo two-photon imaging. AAV delivery and cranial window preparation enabled fluorescence imaging of the experimental readouts in the living brain.</p><h3>Workflow</h3><figure class=workflow><img src=/images/nibs/workflow-imaging.png alt='Original PPT illustration of stereotaxic injection, cranial window, and two-photon imaging'><figcaption>AAV delivery → cranial window → in vivo two-photon imaging.</figcaption></figure><h3>Result · Two-Photon Imaging</h3><figure class=videofigure><video controls muted playsinline preload=metadata poster=/images/nibs/cd39-video-poster.png><source src=/images/nibs/cd39-imaging.mp4 type=video/mp4>Your browser does not support embedded video.</video><figcaption>Unexpected ring-like fluorescence signals were observed during two-photon imaging.</figcaption></figure><h3>Checking for Spectral Crosstalk</h3><p>The unexpected pattern prompted consideration of possible spectral crosstalk under 920-nm two-photon imaging. Local photobleaching of red fluorescence was one possible explanation for the central dark region.</p><div class=results><figure><div class=channel>Green channel</div><img src=/images/nibs/slide-23-image-3.png alt='Green fluorescence channel'></figure><figure><div class=channel>Red channel</div><img src=/images/nibs/slide-23-image-4.png alt='Red fluorescence channel'></figure><figure><div class=channel>Merge</div><img src=/images/nibs/slide-23-image-5.png alt='Merged fluorescence channels'></figure></div><figcaption>Experimental-group images showed less spatial colocalization than the control example. Spatial overlap between channels and unexpected fluorescence patterns highlighted the need to consider spectral crosstalk and possible photobleaching when interpreting ATP-related signals.</p></div></section></div>
